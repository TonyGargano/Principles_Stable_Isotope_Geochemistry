A solar system timeline

Where my research sits in 4.6 billion years of solar system, Earth, and ocean history.

One timeline, from the stardust that predates the Sun to the water cycle running today. The orange band marks stages of solar system history and the blue band stages of Earth and ocean history; the numbered purple pins mark where my research sits in that story. The first 170 million years are stretched, because that is when most of the solar system's chemistry was set. Click a pin or a chip to see what happens at each stage and what I measure there.

Stardust before the Sun

before 4.567 Ga cosmochemistry

The stage. Generations of stars seeded the Sun's parent molecular cloud with dust over billions of years of galactic chemical evolution. A small fraction of those grains survives unaltered inside the most primitive meteorites, condensed around dying stars before the Sun existed, and their oxygen isotope ratios (¹⁸O/¹⁶O and ¹⁷O/¹⁶O) are unlike anything that formed here.

My work here

I model how presolar oxide grains, strongly enriched in ¹⁷O, were inherited unevenly across the young disk, and whether that inheritance, rather than disk photochemistry, set the oxygen isotope diversity of solar system materials.

The Sun's oxygen and the first solids

4.567 Ga cosmochemistry

The stage. The cloud collapsed into a star and a disk. Calcium-aluminium-rich inclusions, the oldest dated solids, condensed at 4.567 Ga, and chondrules followed within a few million years. Meteorites split into two isotopic families, the non-carbonaceous and carbonaceous reservoirs of the inner and outer disk.

My work here

Treating the Sun as the mass-weighted centre of the solar system's oxygen inventory, I ask what mixture of gas, dust, and ice gave each body its δ¹⁸O and δ¹⁷O, and why inner solar system materials sit displaced from the primitive mineral line that anchors early solar system oxygen.

Volatiles leave the rock

~4.57-4.55 Ga cosmochemistry

The stage. Planetesimals accreted within the first few million years and were heated from inside by ²⁶Al decay. Moderately volatile elements, the alkalis, the halogens, sulfur, were evaporated, redistributed, or locked into new phases, and the most reduced bodies grew sulfides where other bodies grew oxides.

My work here

Halogen abundances (F, Cl, Br, I) with Cl and K isotopes across ordinary and enstatite chondrites, arguing that parent bodies distilled their own volatiles rather than inheriting a nebular depletion, plus Fe and Mg isotope work on evaporation, condensation, and sulfide formation in enstatite chondrites.

Water on the first worlds

~4.56-4.50 Ga cosmochemistry

The stage. Ice accreted into chondrite parent bodies melted, and the water reacted with rock, leaving carbonates, clays, and magnetite behind. The solar system's first aqueous chemistry ran inside asteroids, and it also set how oxidized their silicates became.

My work here

δ¹⁸O and δ¹⁷O of CM chondrite carbonates as a record of the fluids they grew from, and a closed-parcel Fe + H₂O mass balance for how ice content and hydrogen set the oxidation state of early solar system silicates.

The Moon's record

~4.50-4.44 Ga, archive to today lunar

The stage. A giant impact formed the Moon around 4.5 Ga (the exact age is debated). Airless and dry, its surface then ran for billions of years as a passive recorder, collecting impacts, solar wind, and infalling dust in its regolith.

My work here

Chlorine isotopes and volatile loss from lunar materials, Apollo 16 regolith and impactites, and triple oxygen isotopes (δ¹⁸O with Δ′¹⁷O) of regolith to weigh the impactor dust raining on the Earth-Moon system and ask where that compositionally diverse dust comes from.

Seawater's ingredients

~4.4-3.8 Ga Earth

The stage. Zircons at 4.40 Ga point to crust interacting with liquid water, and oceans were likely in place by roughly 4.3 Ga (inferred, debated). When the water arrived, its dissolved load arrived with it, and seawater chemistry has been evolving with crust and climate ever since.

My work here

The bulk-Earth halogen budget, where the Cl now residing in seawater and crust came from and what its isotopes say about delivery during accretion, and the same carbonate-fluid oxygen isotope tools (¹⁸O/¹⁶O and ¹⁷O/¹⁶O together) that I use to read asteroidal waters, applied to waters on Earth.

Dolomite seas

Proterozoic to today Earth

The stage. Dolomite is abundant through the older carbonate record yet barely forms in modern oceans, a long-standing puzzle. Every dolomite grew from some water, evaporated seawater, burial brines, lake waters, hydrothermal fluids, and the rock keeps that water's fingerprint.

My work here

The diversity of dolomitizing fluids. Reading δ¹⁸O and δ¹⁷O together, with carbon isotopes alongside, to ask which waters actually made the dolomites we have, and how often the answer differs from the seawater default.

Water, air, and breath

today modern waters

The stage. The modern water cycle fractionates isotopes at every step, evaporation from the ocean, cloud processes, rain, re-evaporation. Animals then fold that signal into themselves, building body water from what they drink, what they eat, and the oxygen they breathe.

My work here

Deuterium excess and ¹⁷O excess, always read with δ¹⁸O, as proxies for aridity, and the fluid-dynamical complications between the signal and the archive; the amount effect in precipitation; and body water and respiration as a recorder of climate and physiology.

Ages. Published values, rounded.
