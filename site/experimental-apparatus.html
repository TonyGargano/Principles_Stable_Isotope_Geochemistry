Building the machines

The hand-built machines of experimental geochemistry, and the questions each one answers. After Holloway & Wood (1988).

The classic machines of experimental geochemistry are simple instruments, built from steel rod, ceramic tube, wire, and glass, and each one is matched to a kind of question. Gas-melt exchange at one atmosphere, fluid-rock reaction at pressure, redox control, sulfidation. Each panel covers one machine, what it is built from, its working range and precision as Holloway and Wood (1988) list them, and the questions it is suited to, with the source figure and page cited.

The machines

Quench and gas-mixing furnace

gas-melt redox Published design
Range
vacuum to 1 bar; to 1,600 °C (p. 18)
Precision
about 1% in P; 1 to 15 °C (p. 18)
Sample
about 0.1 ml, hung on a Pt wire loop (pp. 19-20)
Built from
ceramic muffle tube, resistance windings, insulation, two gas flowmeters, a mixing chamber, Pt wire
Original design
Darken & Gurrey (1945); applied to magmas by Osborn (1959) (p. 19)
Suited to
liquidus relations in iron-bearing systems, mineral and glass synthesis, element partitioning (p. 20)
In the book
entry pp. 18-22; schematic Fig. 3.3, p. 21

What it does. Two feed gases, CO-CO₂ or H₂-CO₂, are mixed at set flow ratios and flowed over the sample, fixing ƒO₂ through the gas equilibrium (pp. 21-22, 172). A current pulse melts the fine quench wire and the sample drops into a bath, quenching in seconds, with nearly zero iron loss to containers and several samples per run (pp. 19-21).

Vacuum evaporation furnace

gas-melt · volatility My bench
Range
below about 10⁻⁶ bar; to silicate liquidus temperatures and beyond
Built from
a vacuum chamber, a pumping stack, W or Mo heater and fixtures, a thermocouple or pyrometer, power feedthroughs
Suited to
evaporation and condensation kinetics, volatile loss from melts and regolith, kinetic isotope fractionation
Provenance
my bench

What it does. Free (Langmuir) evaporation into vacuum. With no returning gas flux, the evaporating surface sets the chemistry, and the escaping vapor carries a kinetic isotope fractionation that the condensed residue records.

Cold-seal vessel, the Tuttle bomb

fluid-rock Published design
Range
1 to 12,000 bar; to 950 °C, inversely linked (pp. 25, 27)
Precision
0.1 to 1% in P; ±1 to 30 °C (p. 25)
Sample
0.1 to 0.5 ml welded capsules (p. 25)
Built from
one superalloy rod (Stellite-25 or Rene-41) drilled nearly through, a cone seal, a water line, a gauge and hand pump, a Kanthal-wound tube furnace (p. 27)
Original design
Tuttle (1949); 12 kbar version Luth & Tuttle (1963) (p. 25)
Suited to
granite and metamorphic phase equilibria, hydrothermal mineral synthesis, diffusion and fluid-phase chemistry (p. 27)
In the book
entry pp. 25-27; schematic Fig. 3.5, p. 26

What it does. The book ranks it the simplest and least expensive of the common high-pressure systems (p. 27). The seal sits outside the furnace, hence cold-seal, so a simple cone seal survives. This is the machine of Tuttle and Bowen's granite melting experiments and the long-standing workhorse of metamorphic and hydrothermal synthesis work (p. 27).

Rocking autoclave with gold-bag sampling

fluid-rock Published design
Range
1 to 2,000 bar; to 600 °C (p. 22)
Precision
0.02% in P; ±10 °C (p. 22)
Sample
about 250 ml of fluid plus rock powder (p. 22)
Built from
an externally heated steel vessel, a flexible gold (now titanium) sample bag, a gold-filtered sampling line and valve, a rocking drive (pp. 23-24)
Original design
Dickson et al. (1963); current form Seyfried et al. (1979); fixed-volume variant Barnes (1963) (pp. 22, 24)
Suited to
hydrothermal alteration of rocks, mineral solubilities, reaction kinetics (p. 24)
In the book
entry pp. 22-25; schematic Fig. 3.4, p. 23

What it does. The rocking keeps fluid and rock stirred, and fluid aliquots are drawn at pressure and temperature through the gold filter, so a single run yields a time series of fluid chemistry. An H₂ membrane reads or sets ƒO₂ (p. 24). Quench takes hours and runs last weeks to months, so it is a machine for fluids, not crystals (p. 24). The Barnes fixed-volume variant samples both liquid and vapor, built for aqueous sulfide work (pp. 24-25).

Internally heated gas vessel

gas-melt Published design
Range
0.1 to 10 kbar; to 1,500 °C (p. 27)
Precision
0.1% in P; ±2 °C (p. 27)
Sample
0.6 to 4 ml, up to 30 ml (p. 27)
Built from
a thick-walled steel vessel with closure heads, an internal double-wound furnace, an argon intensifier chain, optionally a Shaw H₂ membrane (pp. 28-29)
Original design
Yoder (1950); large volume Burnham et al. (1969) (p. 27)
Suited to
melting of hydrous magmas, fluid-melt and fluid-crystal equilibria, synthesizing large volumes of starting material (p. 29)
In the book
entry pp. 27-29; schematic Fig. 3.6, p. 28

What it does. The furnace sits inside the vessel, so pressure and temperature limits are not inversely linked the way they are in a cold-seal. Argon at 5 kbar and 1,000 °C is about as dense as room-temperature water, and its convection is tamed with double windings and vertical operation (p. 29). The machine for volatile solubility in melts and for fluid-melt equilibria needing large fluid volumes (pp. 27-29). The honest caveat, this is the one entry on this page whose pumping system the book concedes is relatively complicated and expensive (p. 27).

Three ways to fix ƒO₂

redox Published
Gas mixing
at 1 atm, ƒO₂ = (PCO₂ / PCO·K)², set by flow ratio (p. 172)
Solid buffers
MH, Ni-NiO, QFM, IW assemblages plus H₂O, sealed in an outer capsule (pp. 172-173)
Membrane
Shaw Pd-Ag tube fed from an external H₂ reservoir (pp. 173-174)
Original designs
gas mixing Darken & Gurrey (1945); buffers Eugster (1957); membrane Shaw (1963, 1967) (pp. 172-173)
Suited to
fixing redox state in any sealed-capsule or gas-flow experiment
In the book
Appendix A.3, pp. 171-174

What it does. In the double capsule, H₂ generated by the buffer diffuses through the Pd-Ag or Pt inner wall and fixes ƒO₂ inside through the water reaction (p. 173). Permeabilities run PdAg > Pt >> Au > Ag (p. 174). Buffers are consumed in long runs and sit at widely spaced ƒO₂ values; the membrane varies ƒO₂ continuously and never runs out (pp. 173-174).

Zirconia oxygen sensor

redox Published design
Range
1 atm; 700 to 1,200 °C (p. 43)
Precision
±0.05 log units in oxygen activity (p. 43)
Sample
small chips or pellets; equilibration in hours (p. 43)
Built from
a Y₂O₃- or CaO-doped ZrO₂ plug sealed into an alumina tube, two Pt-Rh thermocouples, a high-impedance millivoltmeter (pp. 43-44)
Original design
Kiukkola & Wagner (1957); geological use Sato (1971) (p. 43)
Suited to
intrinsic ƒO₂ of rocks and minerals, free energies of redox equilibria (p. 43)
In the book
entry pp. 43-45; schematic Fig. 3.14, p. 44

What it does. Doped zirconia conducts oxygen ions, so the emf across the plug reads the oxygen activity difference between sample and reference through the Nernst relation, E = (RT/4F) ln(aO₂sample/aO₂ref) (p. 45). It measures the intrinsic ƒO₂ of rocks and minerals directly. Watch for iron poisoning of the cell and electronic conduction at very low ƒO₂ (p. 43).

Sealed silica tube

sulfidation Published + constructed
Range
internal pressure set by the charge; usable to the devitrification limit of silica glass near 1,250 °C (Fig. A.1, p. 175)
Built from
a silica tube, a torch, a vacuum line
Suited to
metal-sulfide equilibria, sulfidation of metal and silicates, sealed-tube vapor transport
In the book
one sentence on p. 180, reacting an oxide pellet in an evacuated sealed silica-glass tube when no controlled-atmosphere furnace is available
Extension
the sulfidation arrangement is standard cosmochemistry practice beyond this book, flagged constructed

What it does. Weld one end shut, load the charge, neck the tube, evacuate, and seal with the torch. Sulfur fugacity can be buffered by pyrite-pyrrhotite at one end of a thermal gradient while the sample reacts at the other, and the sealed volume doubles as a vapor-transport cell.

What it is. Every machine above reduces to these parts. The Appendix teaches the parts themselves, which wire to weld, which ceramic survives which atmosphere, which metal passes hydrogen and which blocks it, and how to make a starting material more homogeneous than anything you can buy.
